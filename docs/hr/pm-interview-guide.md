# Project Manager — Interview Guide

**Role:** Senior Project Manager (osaHealth team)  
**Hiring bar:** Best of the best. Targeting someone who has run delivery in high-stakes, technically complex environments and has the scars to prove it. Budget is not the constraint — fit is.  
**Maintained by:** Miriam Kovač, Head of People & Talent  
**Last updated:** 2026-05-26

---

## Comp Band (revised — no ceiling on quality)

| Level | Gross Annual | Target profile |
|---|---|---|
| **Senior PM** | €75,000 – €95,000 | 6–10 years, has built process from scratch in a small tech team |
| **Principal PM** | €95,000 – €120,000 | Deep track record, can own the function and become a hiring manager in 12 months |
| **Lead PM / Head of Delivery** | €120,000 – €140,000 | If the candidate is genuinely exceptional — someone who has led PM functions, not just projects |

We lead with the band in the JD. No "competitive salary." A range.

## Interview Structure

Four rounds plus a written sample. No round without a structured rubric. No debrief before independent scoring is submitted.

| Round | Owner | Format | Duration | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Miriam Kovač | Video call, structured | 45 min | Baseline competency, communication quality, motivation, red flag filter |
| 2 | Vivica Moreno | Delivery problem, scenario-based | 75 min | How they think under ambiguity; problem diagnosis before solution |
| 3 | Michael | Leadership + values | 60 min | Founder-PM alignment; can they push back well? |
| 4 | Async | Written sample, no meeting | — | Coordination quality in writing — the actual daily medium of PM work |
| 5 | Remy Okafor | Technical collaboration | 45 min | Can they hold a technical conversation without translation? Do engineers trust them? |

**Why I added Round 5:** For a best-in-class PM in a technically sophisticated health-data product, the ability to work peer-to-peer with a senior architect matters. It's not a technical interview — it's a trust interview. Remy will know within 15 minutes whether he'd want this person across the table from him in a hard scoping session.

## Round 1 — Screening (Miriam)

### Opening frame
*Tell them the structure, tell them time will be split: half on their background, half on two specific scenarios. This sets expectations and reduces performance anxiety — we're not interested in anxiety as a signal.*

### Section A: Background & Motivation (15 min)

These are exploratory but scored on the quality of thinking, not content.

1. **Walk me through the project you're most proud of in the last three years.** *(Not most successful — most proud of. The distinction matters: pride is about ownership and judgment, not outcomes.)*

2. **Now tell me the one decision in that project you'd change if you could go back.** *(Looking for: specific, honest, demonstrates they understand causality rather than rationalizing outcomes.)*

3. **Why this team, specifically?** *(Not "why PM" — they're already a PM. Looking for: have they done the research? Do they understand what a health-data product with a small, senior team actually requires?)*

4. **What does "delivery" mean to you, in a sentence?** *(Screening question. Candidates who say "getting things done on time and on budget" are telling me they haven't thought hard enough about it. Looking for something that names the human and systemic dimensions.)*

### Section B: Scenario Questions (20 min)

These are SBI-framed — Situation, Behavior, Impact. I write down their answers verbatim and score after the call, not during.

**Scenario 1: The engineer who isn't surfacing the block**
> "You're running a sprint. It's Thursday. Standup has been clean all week — no blockers raised. On Friday afternoon you discover that one engineer has been stuck on a dependency issue since Tuesday and didn't mention it because they 'didn't want to be the bottleneck.' The sprint goal is now in jeopardy. What did you do, and what would you do differently?"

*Looking for: awareness that standups are a lagging indicator; understanding that psychological safety is a PM's problem, not the engineer's; specific change to process or behaviour, not just blame.*

**Scenario 2: The stakeholder who keeps expanding scope**
> "A key stakeholder — not the founder, but someone whose support matters — keeps adding requirements to an already-locked sprint. You've talked to them twice. The behaviour continues. What do you do?"

*Looking for: distinguishes between managing up vs. managing sideways; doesn't collapse under "well they're important"; has a concrete escalation path that isn't just "I escalated."*

### Closing (10 min)

- "What's the one thing you'd want to know about how this team runs before you decide whether to continue?"
- Leave space. The quality of their questions tells you whether they've thought about the job or the title.

## Round 2 — Delivery Problem (Vivica)

### Setup

Vivica runs this as a semi-structured scenario: she presents a realistic situation from the team's actual context (lightly abstracted) and runs it as a working session, not an interview. The candidate should feel like they're in a planning meeting, not on stage.

### Scenario: The slip nobody is naming

> "We're four weeks from a milestone release. The technical architecture is solid. The backend is 80% complete. The frontend is at 60% with three known unresolved questions about offline sync behaviour. There's no formal risk register. The team lead says 'we'll be fine, we've handled worse.' The founder is presenting this milestone to an external partner in six weeks. You've just joined. What do you do in the first five working days?"

*Probing questions Vivica should use if the candidate stays at surface level:*
- "How do you decide what to look at first?"
- "Who do you talk to, in what order, and what are you trying to understand from each person?"
- "The team lead says your risk assessment is too pessimistic. How do you handle that?"
- "What does a realistic updated plan look like, and how do you communicate it to the founder?"

*Red flags:* immediately jumping to tools (JIRA boards, Gantt charts); reassuring the founder before understanding the actual state; treating the team lead's confidence as data.

*Green flags:* asks to see the outstanding questions before deciding anything; names the difference between the stated plan and the inferred reality; says "I don't know yet" at least once and explains how they'd find out.

### Scoring notes for Vivica

Score the **thinking process**, not the final answer. The right answer is unknowable without more information — that's the point. A candidate who produces a confident five-step plan in the first five minutes has failed the scenario.

## Round 3 — Leadership & Values (Michael)

This is Michael's call to run as he sees fit. Suggested areas to cover:

- What's their mental model of a founder-led org? Have they worked in one before? What broke?
- When was the last time they told a founder or senior leader something they didn't want to hear? What happened?
- What does their relationship with ambiguity look like? Not "I'm comfortable with ambiguity" (everyone says this) — a specific story where ambiguity created a real problem and how they navigated it.
- What would they need from Michael specifically to do their best work?

**One strong question:**
> "Describe a time you had to slow something down — push back on urgency — because the urgency was working against the outcome. How did you make the case, and what was the result?"

This surfaces the same judgment I care about: can they distinguish urgency from importance, and can they say so to someone who outranks them?

## Round 4 — Async Writing Sample

Send after Round 2, before Round 3 is scheduled. Give them 48 hours.

**The brief:**

> You are the PM on a product team. A feature your team has been building for six weeks is now showing signs of significant scope creep and a realistic risk of a two-week slip. You need to communicate this to two audiences: (1) your team, who need direction; (2) the external partner stakeholder, who needs to understand the situation without losing confidence.
>
> Write both communications. They should be real documents you'd actually send — not polished exercises. Combined length: whatever it takes, no more.

**What I'm scoring:**

- Does the team message give them something actionable, or just information?
- Does the stakeholder message acknowledge the problem without catastrophizing or glossing over it?
- Are the two messages consistent — the same reality, different framing — or do they tell different stories?
- Is the writing clean? "Please advise" is a hard no. Hedging without information is a hard no.

## Round 5 — Technical Collaboration (Remy)

Not a technical interview. Remy should frame it as a conversation about how they've worked with engineering leads and architects.

**Questions Remy should explore:**

1. "Walk me through a time you had to mediate between what a stakeholder wanted and what the architecture could actually support. What was the gap, and how did you navigate it?"

2. "If I told you we're evaluating a move to an event-sourced architecture for our health-data persistence layer — what questions would you ask to understand what that means for delivery planning?"  
   *(Remy is not looking for the right technical answer — he's looking for whether they ask the right questions. Does 'what does this mean for the team's learning curve?' appear? Does 'how does this affect our MVP timeline?' come up?)*

3. "What would tell you, early in your time here, that engineering trusts you?"

*Red flag:* treats this round as a chance to demonstrate technical knowledge they don't have. Green flag: immediately names that their job is to clear the path, not to solve the technical problem — and can articulate what clearing the path actually looks like.

## Scoring Rubric (all rounds)

Scale: 1–4 only. No half-scores.

| Dimension | Description | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Delivery clarity** | Articulates what "done" means; catches slippage early | Can't define it concretely | Functional definition, missing nuance | Clear, with examples | Would catch problems before they're named |
| **Stakeholder handling** | Manages up/sideways without creating drama | Avoids hard conversations | Gets through them but creates friction | Handles well, specific evidence | Tactful and clear; leaves people better informed |
| **Async communication** | Written quality: clear, actionable, appropriate length | Hard to follow or bloated | Gets the point across with effort | Clean, clear | Exemplary; I'd circulate this |
| **Ambiguity tolerance** | Energised by undefined problems; doesn't need a playbook | Needs full specification | Can handle moderate ambiguity | Comfortable with partial information | Actively creates clarity where others see chaos |
| **Technical vocabulary** | Can talk with engineers without translation | Lost in technical conversation | Follows, rarely contributes | Holds their own; asks good questions | Engineers want them in the room |
| **Self-awareness** | Accurate model of their own strengths and gaps | No awareness of gaps | Acknowledges gaps but abstractly | Specific about limits and how they compensate | Can describe precisely where they need support |

**Hire bar:** Average ≥ 3.2 across all six dimensions; no dimension at 1; unanimous panel agreement.  
**Best-of-best bar:** Average ≥ 3.5; at least two dimensions at 4; no dimension below 2.

---

## Debrief Protocol

1. Each interviewer submits their scorecard independently before the debrief call. No exceptions.
2. Debrief call: Miriam facilitates. Each interviewer states their overall score and the one dimension that most drove it. No one describes the candidate's personality or "vibe" without attaching it to a specific observable behaviour.
3. If there is a gap of ≥ 1.5 on any dimension between two interviewers: surface it, name the specific data points that drove each score, and determine whether the discrepancy is evidence of a real signal or a calibration error.
4. "I just liked them" is not an input. Miriam will ask for the observable behaviour behind it.
5. Decision is by consensus, not majority. If there isn't consensus: one more structured conversation before a final call.

## Candidate Experience Notes

- Response time to every application: within 3 business days.
- We publish the comp band. Candidates who ask why we publish it are usually the ones who've been burned by games — that's a good sign.
- We tell candidates at Round 1 what the full process looks like and approximately how long it takes. No surprises.
- The async writing sample is the most predictive round. If a candidate says they don't have time for it, that's information.
- We do not speed-run the process because we're in a hurry. Urgency-driven corner-cutting is how we end up with a performance management conversation in six months.

## What "Best of the Best" Means Here

Not the most experienced. Not the most decorated. The one who:

- Has been humbled by a real delivery failure and rebuilt their practice from it
- Can hold the line on process when the founder is impatient — because they've seen what happens when they don't
- Makes engineers trust them without having to earn it every week
- Writes things that make other people's thinking clearer, not just their own

The candidate who's right for this team knows exactly why they want to work here and has done the reading. The ones who haven't won't last the writing sample.
